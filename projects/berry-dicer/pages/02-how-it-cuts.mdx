---
title: How it cuts
summary: Pinch the leaves off by hand; the cubes come from the grid's height, its cells and the step.
---

**Hulling is done by hand as each berry goes in: pinch the leaves off and drop it
in the hopper.** Pinching takes the green and very little fruit, about 2 mm off the
length (EST), much less than a straight cut across the shoulder would. A hard white
core stays in. If that bothers you, a huller or the tip of a knife takes it out.

**Dicing: the grid is 12 mm tall with 12 mm cells, and the platen moves 12 mm per
click.** Each click pushes exactly one grid height of fruit out of the bottom, and a
wire swept just under the grid cuts it off as a layer of 12 mm cubes. The tips and
the outside of each berry come out as smaller pieces. The last 12 mm stays in the
grid until the next batch pushes it out.

<details>
<summary>The detail</summary>

- **Orientation.** Once the leaves are off, a berry is just fruit: on its side, its
  cut face or its tip, the cubes come out the same.
- **Why a wire underneath.** A grid alone makes sticks the height of the berry. The
  wire cuts each 12 mm layer off as it comes through, which is what makes cubes.
  Egg slicers cut strawberries cleanly, and a wire has no edge to keep sharp. It is
  0.5 mm stainless (EST gauge) on a stainless bow.
- **Why a strip grid, not a wire grid.** A wire grid would sag under the press. The
  strips are 0.030 in stainless with their top edges sharpened on a whetstone.
- **Stem stubs.** A long stem left on a berry can prop it up in the hopper. It
  still cuts; the stub ends up in one cube.

</details>

## What else it cuts

**Anything soft enough to push through by hand comes out as 12 mm cubes.** The
limits are how hard the food is to cut, and how tall it is: the hopper holds about
55 mm above the grid.

| Food | Likely | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Strawberries, mango, melon, kiwi, peaches | Yes | What it is sized for. Peel and stone first |
| Cooked potato, sweet potato, beets | Yes | Cool them first so they hold together |
| Cucumber, courgette, mushrooms | Yes | Cut to fit under 55 mm |
| Tomatoes | Messy | Soft skin tears before it cuts; ripe ones squash |
| Firm cheese, tofu, cooked chicken | Probably | Cheese sticks in the cells: oil the grid |
| Onions | Yes, but | The layers come apart into flakes, not cubes |
| Raw potato, carrot, apple | Not a full load | Much harder to cut (not measured). Try a few pieces |

**The force depends on how much of the grid is in use.** The 27 berries use about
half of it. A full layer of food covering the whole grid needs about twice the
handle force: 72 N at the berry cutting figure (EST). Harder foods scale from there,
so for raw roots press a few pieces at a time.

<details>
<summary>The detail</summary>

- **Force.** The handle force is the cutting force per millimetre of edge, times the
  edge length in the cut, over the 14 to 1 handle. The grid has 2A/c of edge in a
  section of area A. A full 312 × 156 mm layer is 8100 mm of edge: 811 N on the
  platen and 72 N on the handle at 0.1 N/mm. At 0.5 N/mm that is 360 N on the
  handle, which no one can turn.
- **Harder foods.** The cutting force for raw potato or carrot is not measured here.
  It is several times the berry figure (EST), so the advice is a few pieces at a
  time. Weigh one on a kitchen scale the same way as a berry (see
  [what to measure](../measure/)).
- **Other cube sizes.** The cube size is the grid, the step and the wire together.
  A 10 mm grid would need a different pinion or index wheel too. The press is
  modeled with only the 12 mm grid.

</details>
