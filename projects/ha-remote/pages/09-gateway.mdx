**A third kit in a 74 × 42 × 18 mm box on the office desk, plugged into USB and
always listening.** It turns every LoRa packet from the remotes into the same Home
Assistant event the Wi-Fi remotes send, and blinks for each one. The left end is a
vented chamber for an SHT40, so the office gets a temperature and humidity reading
for $6. Modeled, not built. The firmware compiles.

## Only needed for LoRa

**Remotes on Wi-Fi do not use the gateway.** Switch a remote to LoRa and it needs
one gateway within range. A single gateway serves every remote and sensor module
in the house. The office suits it because it is central and already has power.
For the longest range, put it high on a shelf, not under a monitor or against
metal.

<details>
<summary>Why the antennas go where they do</summary>

- **LoRa** runs along the inside of the front wall: 45 mm of flexible antenna,
  lying flat like the ones in the remotes. Matching their orientation helps a
  little. Inside a house the signal bounces enough that it matters less than height.
- **Wi-Fi** is on the right end wall, away from the LoRa antenna and clear of the
  board.
- If range falls short, a U.FL-to-SMA lead and an external 915 MHz antenna are the
  usual fix. The kit's own pigtail would reach a hole drilled in the back wall. Not
  modeled.

</details>

## The sensor chamber

**The SHT40 sits in its own compartment, walled off from the board and vented on
three sides and the top.** Air comes in low at the side and rises past the sensor.
The board runs Wi-Fi all the time and warms the box, so the reading will still sit a
little above the room. The firmware has an offset to set after comparing with a
thermometer.

<details>
<summary>The detail</summary>

- **Wiring.** Four wires from the sensor's header to the XIAO: VIN to 3V3, GND to
  GND, SDA to D4, SCL to D5. They are the same pins as the
  [sensor module](../sensor-module/). They pass through a notch at the top of the
  partition. Plug the notch with a bit of foam or tape so the board's air stays out.
- **How much warmer.** Unknown until measured. The board draws about 0.3 to 0.5 W
  (EST) and heats the main compartment, and the partition passes some of that
  through. A sensor on a short lead outside the box is the fallback if the offset
  drifts with the load.
- **Without the sensor**, leave the board out and delete the `i2c:` block and the
  `sht4x` sensor from `gateway.yaml`. The chamber stays empty.
- **The board's outline** (25.4 × 17.8 mm) and pin order (VIN, 3V, GND, SCL, SDA) are
  estimates: Adafruit's page gives neither.

</details>

## What it reports

**One event per remote press and one per sensor-module reading, plus four sensors
of its own.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `esphome.remote_button` | `remote`, `button`, `battery_v`, `rssi`. The same as Wi-Fi presses, plus signal strength |
| `esphome.remote_sensor` | `node`, `temperature`, `humidity`, `battery_v`, `rssi`, from a LoRa [sensor module](../sensor-module/) |
| Office temperature, Office humidity | From the SHT40, once a minute |
| Last packet RSSI | Signal strength of the last packet heard, any sender |
| Wi-Fi signal | Every five minutes |

<details>
<summary>Packets and the radio settings</summary>

- Packets are variable length with an explicit header. The remotes send 6 bytes
  starting 0xA5 and the sensor module sends 9 starting 0xA6. The gateway drops
  anything else.
- Remotes and gateway must agree on frequency, bandwidth, spreading factor, coding
  rate, preamble and header mode. They live in `common/kit.yaml` and `gateway.yaml`,
  so change both together. The defaults are 915 MHz, 125 kHz, SF7, 4/5.
- Packets are not encrypted or signed. Anyone with a LoRa radio nearby could
  replay a press. Keep LoRa remotes off locks and garage doors.

</details>

## Printing

1. **Shell and light pipe: one print, top face down.** The vents are vertical
   slots, so they need no bridging. The light pipe is clear PLA, printed with
   the top.
2. **Base: floor down.** It carries the partition, the kit cradle and the sensor
   rails, with TPU feet and grip strips.

The base hooks into the shell at the sensor end with a tongue, and four M2 screws
hold the rest. Power comes from any 5 V USB adapter through a USB-C cable out
of the back.
